GG EZ Game Design Document
GG EZ is a short fighting-game prototype about the impulse to insult a stranger after losing. The
current build contains a repeatable match, reply and room-visit loop. The thirteen-scene story is a
production plan, not a playable campaign.

Carl Kho and Dain Kim · 31 October 2026 · Checkpoint Game Jam, theme: Play to Prevent.

Implementation baseline: main.gd at commit 96537b5 . Story sources: docs/storyboards/ACT3-
TABLES.md , docs/storyboards/ACT45-TABLES.md and their linked boards. Repository: https://
github.com/CarlKho-Minerva/gg-ez.


1. The player loses, then gets three seconds to reply
You lose an online fighting match. The opponent types “ez”. Two replies appear beside a three-second
clock. Letting it expire is a third choice: silence.

The intended checkpoint is the return of that same choice after seeing the other person’s room. We
use “checkpoint” here as our working design term for a pause in an automatic hostile response. The
story asks whether knowing something about a stranger changes what the player presses. It does not
claim that a game about trash talk prevents radicalisation.

The planned opening and final chat have the same framing, wording and reply window. The approved
storyboard pair is “okay that was clean” and “fuck off”. The runtime still uses “wow, you’re good” and
“fuck you” ( OPT_NICE , OPT_MEAN ). Those quoted insults are game content, not the document’s voice.

The build records choices and elapsed reply times. No player study or evidence of lasting behaviour
change is presented here. A different reply within one run would be a description of that run, not proof
of prevention.


2. The current build repeats a rigged match
The runtime is a rage loop, not the story’s three-match arc. Its thresholds come from main.gd ; its
overall play length is unmeasured.

1. Fight. Move, jump and attack in a side-view arena. The move table assigns damage ranges from 2
   to 7 percentage points ( MOVES ). At 90 percent opponent damage, an attack input is delayed by
   0.45 seconds and an in-range attack misses once. Lia starts firing a decisive attack at that
   threshold or after the 30-second match timer. She keeps firing until it lands ( _update_you ,
   _attack , _update_her ), so 30 seconds is not a measured hard limit on the round.
2. Reply. After the knockout, Lia taunts the player. The reply window is 3 seconds ( REPLY_WINDOW ).
   Silence is recorded when the next update notices expiry; the stored time is actual elapsed time, not
   fixed at exactly 3000 milliseconds ( _on_choice ).
3. Rematch or leave. RAGE QUIT appears after 3 losses since the last room visit. The controller is
   thrown automatically after 5 ( RAGE_AFTER , FORCED_AFTER ). Its impact cracks the screen and
   reveals her room.
4. Look around. Click room objects to read a line about them. Later visits expose more objects. The
   phone opens a conversation. Her portrait and screen then show the other side of the exchange
   before another queue.


                              GG EZ, game design document, 2026-10-31, page 1
5. End. Two consecutive kind replies after at least one room visit, 8 losses, or “log off” lead to an end
   card and feedback form ( nice_streak , visits , MAX_LOSSES ). The first kind reply is deliberately
   shown as unsent and does not count toward the streak.

The last rule matters: the runtime rewards a kind-reply streak by ending the loop. The proposed ending
instead avoids an empathy score or a labelled correct answer. They are different designs.

HANDOFF.md records an earlier end-to-end self-playing test at commit d2b3970 . That historical test
does not certify the current build or the planned scenes.

Three rules for the proposed story
 • The ghost observes, never intervenes. It cannot touch anything, speak to Lia or fix her
   circumstances. She never knows it is there. The current runtime has GHOST = false ; it shows the
   other side without implementing this character.
 • One faulty connection links both rooms. The broken screen sends the player into her room;
   another queue brings them back. There is no separate afterlife explanation.
 • Winning is independent of empathy. The planned final match allows either outcome.
   Understanding Lia is not a combat upgrade. This is not true of the current rigged-match loop.


4. Lia is competitive before she is sympathetic
In the proposed story, Lia (lia_ph) lives in Lapu-Lapu City and takes customer calls remotely during a
night shift. She games during a scheduled break. She is skilled, funny, competitive and sometimes
inconsiderate. Her working conditions explain the evening without excusing every action.

Her voice depends on who she is addressing: gamer slang to strangers, Cebuano to family, scripted
English to customers. The runtime still says Quezon City and uses the older phone copy. The draft
Cebuano and customer-call dialogue need independent language and workplace review before they
are treated as finished.

The player is a username whose character comes from their choices. Dain’s proposed fighters are a
wolf for the player and a wizard for Lia. The ghost retains the wolf’s ears and muzzle but replaces its
legs with a floating tail. Those drawn designs and the ghost are intended replacements, not a claim
about the generated runtime art.

5. Repeat the frame so the person changes its meaning
The staging uses SANABI as a composition reference, credited to WONDER POTION and NEOWIZ.
These are our design choices, not verified quotations from its director. The local storyboard tables
describe how to apply them.

1. Establish the arena wide. Move closer when frustration becomes the subject.
2. Give the chat the same crop and scale at the opening and ending. Do not announce a correct
   answer with a new colour or celebratory effect.
3. Match the monitor’s position across the crossing. Let the fan be heard before the room is revealed.
4. Show Lia’s humour before work pressure. The first visit is not a tour of hardship.
5. Repeat the wide room on the second visit. Changed posture and voice should explain the change
   before the camera moves ( ACT3-TABLES.md , V2.2).
6. Hold after the call; avoid a dramatic camera move on every line. Return wide when the work
   resumes (C1.8, C1.13).
7. End with her life continuing. A polite reply does not fix her shift ( ACT45-TABLES.md , E.3–E.7).

The proposed palette separates the vivid arena from the warm bedroom and cooler work-screen light.
The room should not become colourless when the subject turns serious. Sound follows the same
distinction: arcade noise gives way to fan and room tone, then to the work call. These are staging
targets, not claims that every cue is implemented.

screen crack and two Lia portraits. assets/sfx/ contains effects and room, screen and street
ambience. Their presence does not establish a finished cinematic sequence.

7. Test whether the pause reads as a choice, not a lesson
The design has moved from a short story proposal to a repeatable prototype and editable scene
boards. That is evidence of iteration, not evidence that the intended audience finds it effective. No new
participant feedback was collected for this document.

The next human playtest should ask whether the loss feels unfair but understandable, whether the
reply window allows a deliberate choice, and whether the room reveals a person rather than a moral
lesson. Observe where players hesitate or become confused, then revise the relevant beat. Review the
dialogue with a Cebuano speaker and someone familiar with Philippine customer-support work. Those
are planned checks, not completed reviews.

Choice logs can describe a before-and-after within one session. They cannot establish lasting change,
distinguish empathy from learning how to end the loop, or remove the bias introduced by the first
unsent kind reply. The feedback form is self-report. Claims about prevention require evidence beyond
this prototype.
