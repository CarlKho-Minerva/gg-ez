GG EZ — Complete Game Storyboard

Story Direction v0.3 · Carl Kho × Dain Kim · SANABI-inspired 2D cinematic narrative game

10

I think GG EZ should borrow the thing that makes SANABI emotionally effective: the player becomes attached to a character through small actions and repeated interactions before the story asks them to feel something larger.

SANABI director You Seung Hyeon described a story-first process of writing scenes, testing them with temporary artwork, and then refining animation and cinematic camera work. That's the approach I'd use here: first design the emotional sequence, then decide which moments deserve animation and close-ups.
I.N.T

For GG EZ, there's an especially useful twist: the player spends the entire game beside Lia, but Lia never knows they exist.

This version establishes three story rules:

The ghost is completely intangible and invisible to Lia. It can move, observe, and pass through physical objects, but can't touch anything, speak to her, or change her circumstances.

The fighting game and the bedroom share a supernatural connection. A broken screen sends you into Lia's world. When she queues for another match, that connection pulls you back.

Winning is independent of empathy. The player can win or lose the final fight regardless of what they discovered. Understanding Lia isn't a power-up.

I'm also making one continuity adjustment to the earlier draft: Lia works a remote night shift, so the player can observe her actual BPO work rather than relying entirely on recorded calls. Her first gaming session happens during a scheduled break. The second visit follows her as she returns to work. We can show a later break with a short time transition for the final match.

This is a proposed story decision, not something verified in your inaccessible GitHub notes.

Why this is the emotional centerpiece

We've heard two versions of Lia speaking to strangers.

During the game:

“ez”

At work:

“I understand why you're frustrated.”

The contrast isn't simply that one is bad and one is good.

At work, she is expected to be calm, patient, helpful, and professionally pleasant even when someone is angry with her. During her break, she gets to drop that performance and act like a competitive gamer.

There's also a contradiction: Lia knows firsthand how it feels to have a stranger direct their anger at her, yet she sometimes deliberately provokes strangers online.

We shouldn't resolve that contradiction for the player. It's one of the most interesting things about her.

SANABI-style staging

I would avoid a long cutscene where Lia tells us how she feels.

Instead:

Show her sitting upright when the call connects.

Make her portrait smile politely while the customer is speaking.

Briefly show her hands stopping over the keyboard.

After the call ends, hold a close-up on her tired eyes.

Return to the wide room with the ghost floating silently beside her.

The same character is being expressed through tiny changes in movement, not a dramatic speech.

Art direction and scene production bible

Now that we've established the full narrative, here's how I'd translate it into SANABI-inspired visual storytelling without making the project unnecessarily large.

1. Three recurring visual motifs

The same gesture, different meaning

Lia leans back after a victory in Scene 4, then repeats the motion after a difficult call in Scene 9. Reuse the animation with different facial expressions and timing.

This is my favorite opportunity for emotional storytelling without words.

Portraits reveal what sprites can't

Use expressive Lia portraits above a dialogue box at the bottom of the screen. The portrait conveys her private reactions while her small in-world sprite continues its animation.

Return to the same camera angle

The first and last shots of Lia's bedroom should be almost identical. The room hasn't transformed. What has changed is the player's understanding of it.

2. Lia's character art sheet

I'd prepare five portrait expressions and six small sprite animations.

Smug

Victories, trash talk

Laughing

Jokes, Mika, mistakes

After a difficult call

Family and friendship

The ghost

Floats, startles, sulks, observes

These are exploratory visual references. The final artwork should use one consistent, original character design and palette.

For in-world animation, focus on Lia sitting, gaming, celebrating, eating, adjusting her headset, and leaning back. She doesn't need a complete walking animation set because most of the story happens around her workstation.

The ghost needs only floating, turning, startled, angry, and idle animations.

3. The dialogue presentation

This is where I would most directly borrow SANABI's cinematic feel.

LIA

“At least dito, alam kong magaling ako.”

At least here, I know I'm good at something.

The final game should preserve natural Taglish as the primary character voice, with readable English translations available where needed.

I wouldn't translate away “Grabe,” “Nak,” “Opo,” or similar expressions. They help establish how Lia speaks to different people. Her register should change naturally between gamer friends, customers, and her mother.

4. What branches, and what remains fixed?

The full story shouldn't become a maze. The five acts form one fixed spine, with exploration and response branches.

6. The Checkpoint Game Jam connection

The official jam design resource defines a checkpoint as a gameplay intervention that interrupts a harmful path and shifts the player's perspective.
Checkpoint Game Jam

Our strongest checkpoint is not the ghost transformation by itself. It's the return to the same three-second response after the player has experienced the other side of the interaction.

The ghost sequence interrupts automatic retaliation by supplying context. The final decision tests what the player does with that context.

That's a credible contribution to pro-social behavior and digital resilience. We shouldn't claim one game about trash talk directly prevents radicalization; we should make the narrower, defensible case that GG EZ lets players practice perspective-taking and de-escalation in a familiar online environment.

My final narrative recommendation

The one scene I would protect even if you and Dain had to cut half the game is Scene 8: Lia taking the customer call.

The second-most important is Scene 13: returning to the same reply prompt.

Everything between those scenes should help the player connect the person who types ez to the person who says “I understand why you're frustrated.”

That's the emotional mechanism.

And the biggest production opportunity from SANABI isn't merely 16-bit artwork. It's the deliberate reuse of gestures, environments, and camera framing so that ordinary moments become emotionally significant later.

I'd treat this storyboard as the narrative master draft for now. Twine can be used to playtest the dialogue and branching; Godot can subsequently implement the side-scrolling presentation. The uploaded #breakitdown framework is useful here too: define each scene individually, but test the connections between them, because the meaning of each discovery depends on what the player has already witnessed.

One final caution before locking the script: We should have someone familiar with Philippine BPO work review the shift, QA, and customer-call dialogue for authenticity, and have a Tagalog speaker review the final Taglish. Those details will determine whether Lia feels like a specific Filipina character rather than a generalized representation of hardship.
