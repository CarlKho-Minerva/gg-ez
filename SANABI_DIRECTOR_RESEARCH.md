## Primary source

[I.N.T interview with You Seung Hyeon](https://int-magazine.com/interview/you-seung-hyeon-of-sanabi/), English version dated 2025-09-08. Interviewers: Jini and Daichi Saito. Read the sections “Art and Camera Work” and “The Story and The Overseer.” The interview contains major SANABI spoilers.

Paraphrased findings:

- The director describes script → temporary art → in-game testing as the production cycle.
- Film shot selection and editing informed the staging.
- Closer camera placement intensifies emotional moments; wider framing creates distance.
- Character animation and backgrounds had distinct art owners. Camera staging helped make their work effective.
- Everyday Korean views informed the setting rather than generic cyberpunk imagery.
- The team omitted extra world explanations that disrupted the central relationship.
- The Overseer changes both the player's available actions and the relationship between the leads. The director also acknowledges difficulty and clarity problems in that sequence.

These are source findings, not a transcript or quotations attributed from memory.

## Applying it to GG EZ: proposed shot directions

The following are our applications, not claims that the director prescribed these shots.

1. **Establish the arena wide.** The player can read the space and nearly land the winning hit. Move closer only as the immediate frustration becomes relevant.
2. **Hold on the result/chat.** The two reply buttons dominate the same composition at the beginning and end. The change is in what the player knows.
3. **Cross through a matched frame.** The player's monitor and Lia's monitor occupy the same part of the image across the transition. The sound changes from arena effects to the fan before the room is fully visible.
4. **Reveal the whole room first.** Let Lia's joke and failed clip establish a person before isolating work or hardship details.
5. **Headset insert, then return wide.** The work script is information; the return to Lia gives it a human context. Keep the room objects spatially consistent between visits.
6. **Repeat the camera position on visit two.** Start with the same room framing. A professional voice and new posture change its meaning before any new camera move.
7. **Slow push after the call ends.** Move from medium to a closer framing while her shoulders fall. Let the silence land before “Kapoy na kaayo.” Avoid a melodramatic camera move on every line.
8. **Pull back for the dinner joke.** The plate, ketchup, headset and Lia share one frame. The joke is an action in her room, not just a floating caption.
9. **Keep Mama's voice note grounded.** Stay with Lia's reaction and unfinished food. Do not leave the room for a costly family flashback.
10. **Use a two-person phone composition for Mika.** The phone and Lia's face can share the frame. Leave a beat for “Silver medal, then.”
11. **Return to the exact opening chat.** Same crop, same scale, same buttons and timing. Do not announce the right response with a new visual language.
12. **End wide and let her life continue.** The player is a brief encounter. Do not turn a polite reply into a camera-led declaration that her life is fixed.

## What Dain needs per storyboard panel

Scene ID; full spoken dialogue; action between lines; start/end framing; movement and duration as adjustable draft values; sound cue; returning object or composition; source reference; unresolved art gap. Do not replace dialogue with a one-line synopsis.

Use simple blockouts first and test the scene. Camera movement is part of the emotional design; a collection of attractive backgrounds is not a substitute for staging.
